# Welcome

## Welcome

Hello and welcome to the Tangible Mental Health team. This employee handbook defines who we are and how we work together. We will do everything possible to create a fair and productive workplace, but we need your help. We’ve created this handbook to guide you. This handbook isn’t a contract or a guarantee of employment. It’s a collection of our expectations, commitments, and responsibilities. Please read this employee handbook carefully and consult it whenever you need to.

## About Tangible Mental Health

When founding TMH careful thought was given to “what would set this practice apart?”. Ultimately the idea was to create a practice that moves away from solely theoretical and intellectualizing and instead connects clients with their emotions in practical ways. At Tangible Mental Health our Mantra is *Helping people **feel** better.* We place an emphasis on creating healing that our clients can feel by connecting them with their emotions through tangible skills.

Good therapy starts with good therapists. We believe in our team and are selective only to bring in therapists which we believe can be great. Our approach to creating good therapy is to emphasize training and development with all of our therapists. We don’t want perfect therapists, we want therapists who strive to improve for the sake of themselves and their clients. We hope that your experience here will be one of pride, growth, and satisfaction in your work.

# Workplace Policies

## Attendance

TMH expects you to be present during your scheduled working hours. If you face an emergency that prevents you from coming to work, you will be responsible for ensuring any impacted parties (including clients) are notified. All absences should be reported to your direct supervisor via text or email. It is important that we create a calm, consistent environment for our clients and employees. Repeated absenteeism impacts this environment, and may be cause for termination.

## Confidentiality and data protection

We want to ensure that private information about clients, employees, partners and our company is well-protected. Examples of confidential information are:

- Employee records
- Unpublished financial information
- All client information
- Unpublished goals, forecasts, and initiatives marked as confidential

We expect you to act responsibly when handling confidential information.

You must:

- Lock or secure confidential information at all times.
- Shred confidential documents when they’re no longer needed.
- Make sure you view confidential information on secure, password-protected devices only.
- Only disclose information to other employees when it’s necessary and authorized.
- Keep confidential documents inside our company’s premises unless it’s absolutely necessary to move them.

You must not:

- Use confidential information for your personal benefit or profit.
- Disclose confidential information to anyone outside of our company.
- Replicate confidential documents and files and store them on insecure devices.

## Employee relationships

### Fraternization

Fraternization refers to dating or being friends with your colleagues. In this policy, “dating” equals consensual romantic relationships and sexual relations. Non-consensual relationships constitute sexual violence and we prohibit them explicitly. If you start dating a colleague, we expect you to maintain professionalism and keep personal discussions outside of our workplace. You are also obliged to respect your colleagues who date each other. We won’t tolerate sexual jokes, malicious gossip, and improper comments. If you witness this kind of behavior, please report it to your supervisor.

To avoid accusations of favoritism, abuse of authority and sexual harassment, supervisors must not date their direct reports. This restriction extends to every manager above an employee.

## Conflict of interest

Conflicts of interest occur when your interests are placed at odds with the clients or the company’s. Conflicts of interest can have serious consequences or interfere with the care of our clients. We expect you to be vigilant to spot circumstances that create conflicts of interest, either to yourself or for your direct reports. If you are experiencing an ethical dilemma, talk to your direct supervisor and we will try to help you resolve it.

## Crisis: risk of harm

Our ethical responsibility to clients is to maintain confidentiality in all circumstances save when there is a report of child abuse, or a client is determined to be an inherent risk to themselves or someone else. If a client’s risk to themselves or others is serious, foreseeable, and imminent, the ethical responsibility of confidentiality is waived and therapists are responsible for taking appropriate steps to protect the safety of all persons involved.

## Crisis: harm to self

As a clinician, you have a duty to your client to provide a thorough and ongoing assessment of your client's symptomology. Clinicians should screen for suicidal ideation frequently and anytime that depression is indicated by a client. If a client reports suicidal content at any point, whether it be ideation or intention, the clinician should conduct further immediate assessment to determine the risk level of the client.

Clinicians should assess the client’s frequency and severity of suicidal thoughts as well as the imminent risk of the client by determining if the client has a plan, means of completion, and intent to carry out that plan. The Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) is a tool that clinicians can and should utilize to understand the risk of clients facing suicidality.

Regardless of scores shown on the Columbia Suicide Scale or other reported factors, all decisions related to safety and exceptions to confidentiality should be based upon clinical judgment and knowledge of the client, remembering that the client's safety should always remain a top priority.

### Mild risk level

- Passive suicidality
- Thoughts of death without any plans to act on these thoughts.
- {-ory-}{+No history+} of suicide attempts.
- Low risk factors for suicide.

If the client reports frequent suicidal ideation regardless of the level of risk, complete a suicide safety plan with the client. The safety plan can be found in Simple Practice and it is recommended you have the client download the ‘Suicide Safety Plan’ app onto their phone to have the plan readily available. Following completing the plan with the client on their phone, add it to Simple Practice to document completion.

Ensure ongoing reassessments are carried out in future sessions. Adapt the client’s treatment plan to address related concerns.

### Moderate risk level

- Specific thoughts of suicide, including potential methods, but without a definite plan or intent.
- May have a history of suicide attempts or self-harm behaviors.
- Presence of multiple risk factors for suicide.

Discuss any lethal means available to the client as part of the safety plan. Arrange and confirm removal or reduction of lethal means as feasible. This may include asking for permission to contact a loved one of the client to arrange with them the removal or reduction of lethal means. Inform your supervisor of the risk and steps taken to reduce the risk.

Assess that the effectiveness of the current treatment plan and level of care are sufficient to keep the client safe. Make adjustments and recommendations accordingly.

Check in briefly with the client the next business day and assess the current risk level. Ensure ongoing reassessments are carried out in future sessions.

### Severe risk level

- Specific plans for suicide with the intent and means to act on these plans.
- History of previous suicide attempts or serious self-harm behaviors.
- High or escalating risk factors for suicide, including expressions of hopelessness or perceived burdensomeness.

Inform the client of the limits of confidentiality, and encourage them to call a loved one to accompany them to the emergency room or another crisis receiving center. Stay with the client, and do not leave them alone. Remain calm and empathetically engage with the client to encourage them to voluntarily proceed to the emergency room. If a client refuses to take steps for further care, contact emergency contact and/or law enforcement for assistance.

## Crisis: harm to others

In Utah, therapists are held under the duty to warn when a client communicates to the therapist an actual threat of physical violence against a clearly identified or reasonably identifiable victim. If the therapist is made aware of such threats to another person, the therapist should make all reasonable efforts to communicate the threat to the victim and notify a law enforcement officer or agency of the threat. Disclosures of intent to harm from the client and/or the client’s family members should follow the same procedure of notifying the possible victim and law enforcement.

There must be a “serious and imminent threat to harm” to trigger a duty to warn. Unless there is a serious and imminent threat, HIPAA privacy standards still prevail. We have a duty to conduct a risk assessment and thoroughly document what we considered in making that assessment.

Any case that presents with reckless or dangerous behavior that may involve danger to self or others should be brought for consultation. Even if no specific threat is made to another person, consult as soon as possible with your supervisor.

### Mild risk level

- Thoughts of violence without any plans to act on these thoughts.
- No history of violent acts or outbursts.
- Low risk factors for homicidality or violence.

If the client reports frequent homicidal ideation regardless of the level of risk, complete a safety plan with the client. Ensure ongoing reassessments are carried out in future sessions. Adapt the client’s treatment plan to address related concerns.

### Moderate risk level

- Specific thoughts of violence including homicidality, including potential methods, but without a definite plan or intent.
- May have a history of violence or impulsivity.
- Presence of multiple risk factors for violence.

Discuss any lethal means available to the client as part of the safety plan. Arrange and confirm removal or reduction of lethal means as feasible. This may include asking for permission to contact a loved one of the client to arrange with them the removal or reduction of means. Inform your supervisor of the risk and steps taken to reduce the risk.

Assess that the effectiveness of the current treatment plan and level of care are sufficient to keep the client and others safe. Make adjustments and recommendations accordingly.

Check in briefly with the client the next business day and assess the current risk level. Ensure ongoing reassessments are carried out in future sessions.

### Severe risk level

- Specific plans for violence with the intent and means to act on these plans.
- History of previous violence or impulsive behaviors.
- High or escalating risk factors for violence.

Begin by ensuring personal safety, excuse yourself from the client and the room, call 911 immediately and seek help from others in the office or nearby businesses. Wait for authorities to respond and follow their direction. If unable to leave the room, focus on seeking help while actively attempting to calm the client.

If a child reports something to you, then YOU must make the report to the Division of Child and Family Services (DCFS). {-You cannot report to a supervisor and allow the supervisor to determine whether a report should be made.-}{+Your supervisor can guide you through the process, but you make the report yourself. When making the verbal report, always have the person you notify identify himself/herself. The notified person’s name should be noted for documentation purposes.

### Information needed for a report

DCFS requires these three pieces of information in order to open and investigate a case:

- The child’s first and last name
- A location where the child can be found (such as a school, home, or daycare)
- Details of the abuse

Other information that may be helpful to DCFS includes:

- Alleged perpetrator full name and address (if disclosed)
- Names of parents
- Birthdate of child

### Investigation

- It is not the responsibility of therapists or other mental health professionals to investigate the legitimacy of the threats or instances of abuse, investigations are the responsibility of law enforcement.
- To determine whether or not there is reason to believe a threat is credible or that abuse has occurred, therapists may (but are not required to) gather information only to the extent necessary to support a reasonable belief that a reportable problem exists.
- Therapists will not contact the client’s parents, relatives, friends, neighbors, or others for the purpose of discussing a report to law enforcement and potential victim or to attempt to determine circumstances surrounding the threat of harm or abuse.
- (Threats of violence only) Therapists are directed not to contact the client or otherwise give them notice of an investigation regarding threats. All clients are notified of mandatory reporting and limits to confidentiality in the informed consent procedure. It is the responsibility of law enforcement to do all tasks relative to the investigation.
- (Child abuse) Therapists are directed not to contact parents or otherwise give them notice of an investigation. All clients and parents are notified of mandatory reporting and limits to confidentiality in the informed consent procedure. It is the responsibility of law enforcement or DCFS to do all tasks relative to the investigation. Parents who make inquiries to therapists about a report or an investigation should be directed to DCFS.
- Clients who make inquiries to therapists about a report or an investigation should be directed to law enforcement.

## Crisis documentation and failure to report

Following any crisis situation ensure a supervisor is informed and documentation is completed within 24 hours. Document all assessments, risk classifications, decisions made, and actions taken in the client's record. Include details of any safety plans, interventions, and communications with other professionals or emergency services.

Failing to follow proper crisis procedures places our clients at risk and creates liability for the clinician and for Tangible. Clinicians found to have violated crisis policies will be immediately investigated and may face termination.

Clinicians can be held legally responsible for failing to report instances of imminent harm and/or child abuse.
